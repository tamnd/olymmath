It is known for a triangle that if the square of each side is less than the sum of the squares of the other two sides, then the triangle is acute-angled and the orthocenter is inside the triangle. Conversely, if the square of one side is greater than the sum of the squares of the other two, then the triangle is obtuse-angled and the orthocenter is outside the triangle.

Let's call a three-edged solid angle acute or obtuse if all its dihedral angles are acute or obtuse, respectively. It needs to be proven that if the square of the area of each face of a tetrahedron with an orthocenter is less than the sum of the squares of the areas of the other faces, then the tetrahedron has all acute solid angles and the orthocenter is inside the tetrahedron.

If the square of the area of one face is greater than the sum of the squares of the areas of the other faces, then the solid angle opposite this face is obtuse, the others are acute, and the orthocenter lies outside the tetrahedron.

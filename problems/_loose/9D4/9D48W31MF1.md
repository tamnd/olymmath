Find all polynomials with complex coefficients $P$ such that for any rational $q, P(q)$ is rational.

Should we say that the polynomial $P(x)=(x-1)^{n}$ has a single root, or $n$ roots that are the same? To avoid confusion, we will address the case of multiple roots.

Definition 1.19. Let $P \in \mathbb{K}[X], \alpha \in \mathbb{K}$, and an integer $m \in \mathbb{N}^{*}$. We say that $\alpha$ is a root of multiplicity $m$ of $P$ if there exists $Q \in \mathbb{K}[X]$ such that $P(x)=(x-\alpha)^{m} Q(x)$ and if there does not exist $Q \in \mathbb{K}[X]$ such that $P(x)=(x-\alpha)^{m+1} Q(x)$. We say that $\alpha$ is a multiple root if $m \geq 2$.

It turns out that we have a rather practical criterion for recognizing a multiple root.

Definition 1.20. Let $P=a_{0}+a_{1} x+\cdots+a_{n} x^{n} \in \mathbb{K}[X]$. We define the derivative polynomial $P^{\prime}$ by $P^{\prime}(x)=a_{1}+2 a_{2} x+\cdots+n a_{n} x^{n-1}$.

The following proposition, reminiscent of the properties of the differentiation operator on differentiable real functions, is fundamental.

Proposition 1.21. For $P, Q \in \mathbb{K}[X]$, we have:

$$
(P Q)^{\prime}=P Q^{\prime}+P^{\prime} Q
$$

Corollary 1.22. Let $P \in \mathbb{K}[X]$ and $\alpha \in \mathbb{K}$. Then $\alpha$ is a multiple root of $P$ if and only if $P^{\prime}(\alpha)=0$.

You probably found the proof of Gauss's theorem given on pp. $51-52$ somewhat artificial. A more natural proof can be obtained using the method of barycentric coordinates. Conduct this proof. (P. 174.)

We limited ourselves to considering the method of barycentric coordinates on the plane. But the same idea can be applied in space. Then instead of the coordinate triangle of Möbius, a coordinate tetrahedron of Möbius appears; each point $P$ is characterized not by a triplet, but by a quartet of numbers - the four masses that must be placed at the vertices of the tetrahedron so that the center of gravity of the resulting four material points is the point $P$.

We do not dwell on a more detailed consideration of the method of barycentric coordinates. In questions of elementary (Euclidean) geometry, this method does not provide anything new compared to the methods of vector algebra - a discipline that is now part of the university mathematics curriculum. Moreover, it is possible to indicate a fairly simple recipe for translating any proof found using the method of barycentric coordinates into a "vector" proof.

Historically, the method of barycentric coordinates arose before the vector method and served as one of the foundations in the creation of vector calculus.

It should be noted that in one branch of geometry - so-called "projective geometry" - the method of barycentric coordinates is still very valuable and allows solving problems to which the vector method is not applicable.

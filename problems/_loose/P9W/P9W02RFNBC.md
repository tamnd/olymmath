Division of Rectangles and Squares. If a rectangle is divided into two rectangles, it is quite obvious that this configuration arose as a result of one division and could not have arisen otherwise. However, if we divide the given rectangle into three rectangles, no one can guess whether this configuration arose immediately as a result of dividing into three parts or sequentially as a result of dividing the original rectangle into two, and then dividing one of the resulting two rectangles into two smaller ones. We will say that dividing a rectangle into two parts is primary, while dividing it into three parts is not primary. More precisely, we call a division primary if it cannot arise as a result of sequential division (regardless of how it actually arose). This definition was given by Prof. Los, who noted that there are primary divisions into 2, 5, 7, 8, ... parts, and at the same time, there are no primary divisions into 3, 4, and 6 parts. (Let the reader prove that there are no primary divisions into 3 and 4 parts, and find a primary division into 5 and 7 parts; Prof. C. Ryll-Nardzewski proved that there is no primary division into 6 parts.)

$1^{\circ}$. Provide an example of a primary division of a square into 5 equal parts.

$2^{\circ}$. Provide an example of a primary division of a square into 7 equal parts.

$3^{\circ}$. Provide an example of a primary division of a square into 8 equal parts.

A "snake of length $k$" is an animal consisting of an ordered $k$-tuple of cells $\left(S_{1}, S_{2}, \cdots, S_{k}\right)$ in an $n \times n$ grid. These cells must be distinct, and for any $i=1,2, \cdots, k-1, S_{i}$ and $S_{i+1}$ must share a common edge. If the snake occupies the cell group $\left(S_{1}, S_{2}, \cdots, S_{k}\right)$, and $S$ is a cell not occupied by the snake and shares a common edge with $S_{1}$, then the snake can "move" to the cell group $\left(S, S_{1}, \cdots, S_{k-1}\right)$. If the snake initially occupies the cell group $\left(S_{1}, S_{2}, \cdots, S_{k}\right)$ and, after a finite number of moves, occupies the cell group $\left(S_{k}, S_{k-1}, \cdots, S_{1}\right)$, then the snake is said to "turn around". Does there exist a positive integer $n>1$ such that in an $n \times n$ grid, there is a snake of length at least $0.9 n^{2}$ that can turn around?

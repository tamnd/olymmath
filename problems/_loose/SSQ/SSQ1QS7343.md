The centroid of a triangle in a geometric sense is also the centroid in a mechanical sense if we consider the triangle as a thin, homogeneous plane lamina. The mechanical centroid $S$ of the convex quadrilateral $ABCD$ is clearly on the line segment connecting the centroid $S_{D}$ of triangle $ABC$ and the centroid $S_{B}$ of triangle $ACD$, and also on the line segment whose endpoints are the centroids $S_{C}$ and $S_{A}$ of triangles $ABD$ and $CBD$, respectively. Draw $S$ using as few lines as possible, allowing parallel lines to be drawn with a protractor by sliding.

In practical engineering, the following procedure is also used. Divide the sides of the quadrilateral into 3 equal parts and let the order of the vertices and division points along the perimeter of the quadrilateral be: $A, A_{1}, A_{2}, B, B_{1}, B_{2}, C, C_{1}, C_{2}, D, D_{1}, D_{2}, A$. Draw the lines $A_{2} B_{1}, B_{2} C_{1}, C_{2} D_{1}, D_{2} A_{1}$, and let their intersection points be $K, L, M, N$, respectively. Then the intersection of $KM$ and $LN$ is $S$. Show that this procedure is correct. Find a method based on this principle to draw $S$ using as few lines as possible.
